If you're like me, your eyes tend to sorta skip over the equipment section that
comes after armor and weapons. Today, I'm going to have to stop and read it with
more attention, as it's still fairly important here. As I've said before, every
last pound of gear matters in the DFRPG.

The section of Chapter 7 that covers Miscellaneous Gear is split into three
subsections, and we'll cover all three here.

Some pieces of gear in this chapter are described as "basic equipment" for a
certain skill. This means you need them to use that skill normally, and if
you're forced to improvise or go without you'll face a daunting -5 or -10
penalty. Items that are high-quality might give a +1 or +2 instead.

Unlike weapons and armor, each item here gets at most one or two lines about its
function and rules, followed by a cost and weight. They each have their uses,
though.

# Basics

This is the kind of stuff that's relatively inexpensive and available at any
town worthy of the name. There are many small sections here that I will discuss
in summary instead of reprinting the contents of the book.

**Camping and Survival Gear** contains tents, canteens, blankets, backpacks, and
all that good stuff that often gets overlooked. Of note here are the Personal
and Group Basics items, which are an abstract ball of knicknacks that are basic
equipment for Survival. The first one is for a single person, containing stuff
like utensils and some basic fire-making items. The group version has cookpots,
a rope, a utility hatchet, and other things you need to help set up camp for 3-8
people. It weights 20 lbs but you can distribute its weight among several
PCs. These don't include shelter, so you might also want tents and bedrolls or
sleeping furs. This is also where you find trail rations.

**Combat Accessories** has a selection of different quivers, but also a sturdy
belt that can support 2 or 3 melee weapons hanging from it, lanyards to mitigate
those pesky "weapon dropped" rolls, and hobnailed boots that let you ignore poor
footing but make a lot of noise. If you need more than 2-3 weapons ready to draw
at all times, wear more belts! I think that's how D&D 3e's fashion came about.

**Containers** are your chests, barrels and buckets. In my experience you tend
to find those in dungeons more often than you need to buy them. There are also
stats for an empty crystal vial of the sort that usually contains potions, and
which you could use to collect samples of corrosive goo and the like.

**Light Sources** covers expensive beeswax and stinky tallow candles, as well as
lanterns and torches. Detailed rules for lighting are in Exploits.

**Medical Gear** has bandages which are basic equipment for First Aid, a proper
first-aid kit which gives +1, a litter for carrying the wounded, and surgical
equipment that can be used for field Surgery or to remove valuable dead monster
bits without ruining them.

**Miscellaneous Hardware** is, yes, the miscellanea among the
miscellanea. Various ropes, chains, a ladder, and poles in both six- and
ten-foot varieties.

**Musical Instruments** are mostly for bards, and for anyone else who wants to
make noise, which requires no skill. There's a "generic" two-handed stringed
instrument which can be anything from an ancient lyre to a modern acoustic
guitar that's your baseline. Woodwinds are lighter but you can't speak while
using them. A harp or zither is more expensive but can be played quietly enough
to not be heard through a door. And if you're more of a bard-barian type horns,
drums, and bagpipes can be heard for miles!

**Tools** is a large category that has your crowbars, hammers, saws, pickaxes
and shovels. It also has a utility machete that's crappy as a weapon but useful
for cutting foliage, and more specialized things like tongs, scales, and even a
backpack toolkit for field armorers.

**Writing Equipment** has somewhat anachronistic sheets of paper in bundles of
20, a scribe's kit with quill, inkwell and penknife, and a humble piece of chalk
for writing on dungeon walls.

# Special Orders

You can start with these when creating a character, but acquiring them in play
requires finding a specialist vendor that deals with them (alchemist, eccentric
artificer, smuggler, etc.).

**Adventure-Wear** covers a large set of specialized and somewhat anachronistic
fashion, like the Delver's Webbing that works like a modern tactical harness
full of pouches and pockets, or helmet lamps, and potion and scroll belts.

**Camping Gear** has alchemical matches, a compass, and a timed candle that
burns at a precise fixed rate, and special rations. Elven rations are very
expensive but three times lighter. Dwarven ones weight twice as much as the
standard human stuff but give you poison resistance after eating them for a
month or more.

**Combat Accessories** here cover bandoleers for those people who absolutely
must carry 24 throwing daggers on them, a variety of crossbow accessories, and
even lamps and lecterns that you can bolt onto your shield.

**Covert Ops and Security** is known as "thief gear" in other games. A very
large section that includes from your classic set of lockpicks and
trap-disarming tools to dungeon camouflage and both smoke and flash bombs for
those who love a good ninja vanish move. It's also where you find stats for
padlocks, which again are something you will run into in the dungeon more than
you'll need to buy.

**Esoteric Supplies** includes the healing kits for the cleric's and druid's
special skills, and your usual selection of holy symbols and holy water.

**Optics** has a bull's eye lantern that projects a long beam instead of a small
circular light, periscopes, telescopes, and mirrors.

**Scholarly Gear** includes professional cartography kits, waterproof scroll
cases, self-righting hourglasses, as well as manuals and tomes that can let you
roll at default for knowledge skills you don't have, or give a bonus to those
you do.

# Concoctions

These aren't magical, but they follow the same rules as Special Order gear, and
so might not be easily available after character creation.

**Chemicals** are mostly throwable grenades that burst on impact. They can be
filled with acid, alchemist's fire, glue, or visibility dust. There are also
glow vials which illuminate as much as a torch when activated but don't use
fire.

**Natural Preparations** include a selection of drinkable druidic anti-toxins,
and bundles of garlic and wolfsbane for use against vampires and werewolves.

**Poisons** are fantasy poisons that act fast enough to affect combat, dealing
some amount of damage at once when the victim fails a resistance roll. They're
too fast for anti-toxins, which only help against stuff with ongoing
effects. Some are utility compounds that you spread on your bladed weapons,
others are grenades. They range from the very weak to the terrifyingly deadly.
