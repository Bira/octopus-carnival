We already went through the basics in chapter 1, character creation in chapters
2 and 3, and a whirlwind tour of the advantages, disadvantages and skills in
chapters 4, 5, and 6. Now it's time for the chapter covering equipment and
treasure. In this post we'll cover the monetary system and talk a bit about
weapons. In the next ones we'll also cover armor, miscellaneous gear, and magic
items.

Being a GURPS variant that can change whatever they want, DFRPG didn't _have_ to
go hard on the detail for encumbrance and gear. Yet they chose to do so anyway,
because according to our introduction in this chapter resource management is a
traditional part of the genre that many find fun. What corners will you cut in
consumables like arrows, potions and food in order to get that nifty weapon or
armor? What will you choose to leave behind to drag that sack of treasure back
to town? How smug will you feel when you realize you packed everything you
needed and nothing you didn't to that expedition?

If that sounds fun to you, you're in the right game! If it didn't, well, at
least you've been warned. Our first section is about buying and selling gear, so
we're going in order of importance here.

## Money Money Money!

The symbol used for money here is the "$". In standard GURPS they call this the
"GURPS dollar", and explain it means different things in each setting. In the
DFRPG, it means "one copper coin".

Dungeon Fantasy coins are, not coincidentally, identical to the ones used by D&D
and Pathfinder. There are 50 of these chonky disks to the pound no matter what
they're made of. Unlike D&D of PF coins however, their values here increase by a
factor of 20. So if a copper coin is $1, a silver coin is $20, and a gold coin
is $400. Gold coins are also valuable and soft enough that they're commonly cut
into halves, quarters or eights. Gold packs a lot more value into the same
weight, which is why delvers love it so much.

There are coins made of weirder metals with in-between values, but those are not
in this book. You might find them as treasure, though.

Every PC starts with $1000 to buy their starting gear. This is modified by the
PC's Wealth. PCs who have that as an advantage get more, those who have it as a
disadvantage get less. They can also exchange character points for extra money
at $500 per point - a common use for those 5 points obtained from quirks.

### Buying

Item prices assume the most typical conditions possible - a typical sale by an
ordinary merchant at a time and place where there's neither a surplus not a
shortage. Everything costs list price at character creation, and campaign events
might change that.

This does mean we're not assuming "Gold Rush" prices as the default like D&D
does, but you'll still find some items here are ridiculously expensive
anyway. The Exploits book will describe some tricks you can try to get stuff
cheaper.

If you really like an item you can spend character points on the Signature Gear
advantage to give it some narrative protection. That advantage costs 1 point per
$500 of the item's value.

Delvers usually live out of inns and such, so it costs $150 a week to live in
town. Again there are tricks to reduce this in Exploits.

### Selling

You don't need to sell coins, of course - they're already money. Ingots or
unshaped lumps of precious metal are also money, they just make the merchants
and bankers reach for their scales.

Everything else will sell in town for a percentage of its "true" value that's
proportional to the Wealth of the character doing the selling. That trait also
represents the quality of the PC's abstract business contacts, you see.

Average-wealth PCs, the default, sell loot at 40% of its value. Yes, it's
perfectly acceptable for the party to ask their richest member to handle all the
selling. Winning a Quick Contest of the Merchant skill versus a "generic" value
of 15 will add 10% to that value (so from 40% to 50%, for example).

## Weapons

Every single PC is going to need at least one weapon. Which one depends on their
combat skills, and what they can both carry, wield, and afford. This section
goes on to explain weapon stats, and then gives us the big weapon tables.

This is where the character's Damage stat comes into play. It's derived from
Strength and includes two different dice expressions, Thrust and Swing, which is
also the mane of what kind of attack they're for. The two start out pretty close
but Swing increases much faster as you get stronger, to represent the benefits
of the lever effect. A weapon's damage stat is expressed in terms of the
wielder's Thrust (thr) and Swing (sw) values, combined with a damage type.

For example, a sword might do sw+1 cut ot thr+1 imp damage, and when wielded by
a ST 13 character (sw 2d-1 / thr 1d+1) that would become 2d cut or 1d+2
imp. We'll learn more about damage types when we talk about combat.

Weapons also have a ST rating which tells you how strong somoeone must be to use
the weapon. This will only be a problem for human delvers when it comes to the
heaviest of weapons, but halflings likely have to be more watchful here.

For bows and crossbows, you read their ST stat as their _rated strength_. The
value on the table is the weakest possible rated strength for that weapon. They
can be built "stronger" at no extra cost. A character still needs to be at least
that strong to handle the weapon, but any ST they have above that score is
"wasted" and doesn't go into figuring damage or range.

The Melee Weapons Table gives us everything you'd expect out of a dungeon
fantasy game.

- Axes and Maces are cheap and have decent damage but tend to be unbalanced -
  you can attack _or_ parry with them in a given turn, but not both. The
  two-handed varieties have high damage but are more expensive, heavier, and so
  unbalanced you might need to spend a whole turn readying the weapon after
  attacking with it.

- Swords of all kinds are balanced and versatile, with both swinging and
  thrusting attacks. However, they are very expensive. If you don't have the
  cash for a real sword, you can use the same skill with balanced clubs and
  staffs by holding them like swords.

- Knives are cheap, but their short blades give them relatively poor damage and
  a defense penalty. When wielded with fencing skills, their damage remains poor
  but their defensive ability increases by a lot.

- Fencing skills can also be used with several types of specialized sword like
  rapiers or smallswords. These tend to have slightly worse damage than other
  swords and be more expensive, but give fantastic defensive bonuses when used
  with the right skills.

- Spears and polearms have excellent damage-to-price ratios, though most of them
  require two hands to use. Polearms are unbalanced on swinging attacks, though
  those can be devastating. Spears are always balanced, because they only have
  thrusting attacks.

- Staffs can be wielded like swords or spears, and also with their own skill
  that gives a better defensive bonus.

- Flails and nunchakus are hard to use and unbalanced, but they're also hard to
  defend against.

- Funnily enough, Unarmed Attacks also appear on the weapon tables. Bare-handed
  punches and bare-footed kicks are free and accessible to every humanoid
  delver, of course, but you also learn how wearing brass knuckles, metal
  gauntlets, or boots affects their damage.

The Ranged Weapon Table has everything you expect and some things you don't.

- Bows of all sizes have excellent range, with damage ranging from middling to
  great as their size increases. Price also increases accordingly.

- Crossbows are heavier and take longer to reload, but do more damage. You can
  use a crossbow that's stronger than you at no penalty, but it will take a lot
  longer to reload.

- Slings are hard to use but they do damage based on the character's Swing
  instead of Thrust.

- We have many types of thrown weapon: spears, axes, knives, shuriken, darts,
  even boomerangs. Some of these can also be used in melee and appear on those
  tables as well. They tend to have shorter ranges than the dedicated ranged
  stuff.

- Spear-throwers are devices that extend the range of thrown darts and spears
  and let their damage use the PC's Swing value instead of their Thrust.

- And you also have some oddballs: lassos, nets, and blowpipes.

There's a box-out ammunition table here too, containing blowpipe darts, arrows,
crossbow bolts, and several options for slings: unshaped rocks (free, crappy),
shaped rocks, and lead pellets (more expensive, better). There's a type of
crossbow called a Prodd that can also fire lead pellets instead of bolts.

## Weapon Modifiers

Any weapon that's not an uncovered natural appendage can have Modifiers, which
improve its inherent traits and make it cost more. These aren't magic bonuses,
but facts about a weapon's construction. They _can_ live alongside and stack
with more traditional enchantments. Not all modifiers can apply to all weapons.

The modifier categories here are pretty intuitive, but just in case there's a
few paragraphs listing which weapons fall into each one.

Each modifier has a "Cost Factor". To figure out the final price of a modified
weapon, you add all the Cost Factors together + 1 , and then multiply that by
the weapon's base price.

- **Balanced** weapons are more accurate, generally giving a +1 to skill for
  melee weapons and a +1 to Accuracy for ranged ones.

- **Bow-Harps** are heavier than mormal bnows but can be used as either bows or
  harps in any given turn (but not both at the same time!). The perfect gift for
  your ranged bard.

- **Climber's** weapons have a design that let you climb with the weapon ready,
  and give +1 to Climber's skills. Applies to hatchets and other similar
  weapons. I never used this, but Lara Croft swears by them.

- **Dwarven**: Remember those unbalanced weapons that don't let you attack and
  parry on the same turn? That's shoddy human engineering. Good dwarven work
  solves this problem and makes them just as nimble as a sword. Big weapons that
  become unready after an attack still do that, though.

- **Elven**: Applies to bows (not crossbows, the ugly things). Lets you treat
  the bow's rated ST as 2 points higher for calculating damage and range. This
  means a ST 11 character could use a ST 13 elven bow normally. Expensive, but
  your scouts will love it.

- **Fine**: The weapon is sturdier and generally better at its job. Bladed melee
  and thrown weapons, as well as pointy ammunition, do more damage. Bows and
  crossbows shoot farther. All are more resistant to breakage.

- **Meteoric**: One of the game's special materials. Meteoric Iron is immune to
  magic. The weapon ignores any magic meant to stop it and can't be targeted by
  spells, but can't be enchanted either.

- **Orichalcum**: This bronze-colored alloy is indestructible! Weapons made from
  it never break, and have a higher chance of breaking other weapons when those
  rules come into effect.

- **Ornate** weapons are blinged out, and can improve reactions from buyers and
  other people who are impressed by the ostentation.

- **Silver** weapons are useful for exploiting certain monster
  vulnerabilities. Solid silver is best for that, but also results in a fragile
  weapon. A silver coating doesn't affect breakage chances and is far cheaper,
  but is a little less effective at the supernatural side.

- **Very Fine** is like Fine, but has double the bonuses and only applies to
  swords and fencing weapons. Super expensive, of course.

In addition to the type restrictions the Fine, Very Fine, and Solid Silver
modifiers are mutually exclusive. The material modifiers of Meteoric,
Orichalcum, and Solid Silver are also mutually exclusive. Meteoric and
orichalcum weapons can still be silver-coated however, so those players who
immediately went to "electroplating" when they read the material descriptions
still have something to be happy about.

With these modifiers you can have yourself a Fine, Balanced, Dwarven,
Silver-Coated werewolf-hunting axe for $1000, the same price the swashbuckler
pays for a standard-issue edged rapier.
