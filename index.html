    Let's Play Hell's Rebels: Jania Moonshadow update 1

    I’ve been stressing out about real-life fascists these past few days, so I thought I’d destress a bit by publishing another post about people fighting fantasy fascists in my solo Hell’s Rebels campaign

    This is an update to Jania. Her description and backstory remain the same and can be see in the post for her starting version.

    After playing through Adventure 1 I can say Jania has been working pretty much as expected. A thiefy wizard, and a wizard thief. She has handled all the trap-finding and lock-opening that she needed to, and her spells have been occasionally useful as well. The standout here were the repeated uses of Glass Wall to help with the breaching-and-clearing of Hocum’s Phantasmagorium at the end of the adventure.

    I didn’t expect a lot out of her in combat since she’s a Thief-type, but she was surprisingly useful when she got to hit people in the skull with her cane while Art held them down. She’s switching to a smallsword now, so I wonder how that will change things. Her ranged combat on the other hand has been as disappointing as expected, since it takes a while to charge up an Ice Dagger and it often misses or gets dodged.

    Her advancement has been focusing more on utility spells and skills, particularly the ones needed to help run the rebellion. Like the rest of her companions, she started out with 250 points and currently sits at 289 with 0 unspent points. The biggest expenditure here was a much-needed improvement of Spell-Thief Expertise from 2 to 3.

    Let's Read DFRPG Adventurers: Appendices

    Chapter 7 was the last proper chapter in the book but we still have a bit to go through. The first thing here are a pair of sample characters, each taking up a page.

    We have Grükuk Kzaash, a Half-Orc Knight. Personality-wise, she’s a honorable mercenary, if more than a bit short-tempered. Mechanically, she goes for breadth over depth, taking Extra Attack 1 instead of Weapon Master and carrying both a shield and a brace of weapons fit for all occasions. She has a Dwarven flail, a kukri for enemies who are resistant to crushing damage, and a pair of silver-coated brass knuckles for enemies who are vulnerable to that. No swords, though, as she thinks they’re overrated.

    The other one is Francesco Zombani, who already knows all the zombie jokes related to his name and asks that you don’t make any. He’s a former cloistered monk who decided to travel the world on a quest to atone for a grave sin. What sin is that? He’s not actually sure. He’s the robe-and-staff kind of cleric, a poor combatant but an excellent spellcaster.

    After these two we get to the book’s Index, and if you never beheld the index of a GURPS book you don’t know what joy is. This is a match for any textbook index, and leaps and bounds above almost anything else you might find in RPG books. There are also separate indexes for Advantages, Disadvantages, and Skills.

    We end the book with a one-page ad for Ogre 6th Edition, and a four-page character sheet that has enough space to hold almost everything you need in play. Including that price list for magical healing in town I mentioned in the gear chapter.

    Let's Read DFRPG Adventuers: Magic Items

    This is what everyone’s been waiting for, right? In the implied setting of DFRPG, “low-level” magic items are common enough that they can often be found in “magic shops” in cities or larger towns. The selection presented here represents those. There are many more, and more powerful items, but those are not for sale and are usually found in dungeons.

    Potions

    Potions follow the same basic rules as the non-magical concoctions from the previous post, but they’re magic items. This means they can be detected as such by someone with Magery, and their ongoing effects can be ended by Dispel Magic against the skill of the maker. That’s 15 for potions bought in town, but it could be higher or lower for found potions.

    Multiple doses of the same potion usually aren’t cumulative, but different potions can affect someone at the same time.

    This is where you have grenades that are more damaging versions of the alchemical ones, as well as one that puts people to sleep and another one that prevents wizardly casters from using magic for a while. Drinkable potions include a whole range of healing potions, attribute boosters for ST, DX, Basic Speed and Perception, stuff that gives you flight, invisibility or extra DR for a while, and Thieves’ Oil which makes you extra-slippery.

    Scrolls

    Each scroll contains a single use spell. There’s some rules about how long it takes to draw and unroll them in combat, and after that you take twice as long as normal to cast the spell.

    To create a scroll, you decide what spell is inside (pick from any list!) and then whether the scroll is wizardly, clerical, druidic or universal. Universal scrolls can be used by anyone, even a “mundane”. The others can only be used by characters with the appropriate power trait.

    Finally, a scroll can be charged or not. A charged scroll contains all the energy necessary to power the spell, and costs nothing from the reader. An uncharged one drains the reader’s FP. In any case the scroll’s monetary cost scales with its energy cost.

    Delvers prefer universal charged scrolls, of course, but as you might imagine those are the most expensive of all. Further restrictions decrease the cost. Scrolls bought in town have a skill level of 15 both for casting the spell and for resisting dispelling attempts. Found scrolls will often have higher skill.

    Magic Weapons and Armor

    The stars of the show! Weapons and armor most often contain permanent, always-on enchantments, and this is the list of enchantments purchasable by PCs. To create a magic item, you first buy the base “mundane” weapon or armor with all its modifiers, and then apply the enchantments you want to it. These add directly to the final price.

    When a PC buys a magic item in town, they get to decide whether the enchantment is wizardly, clerical, or druidic (which will matter for rules described in DF: Exploits). These purchases can happen either in play or as part of character creation, though few delvers will have the money to spare at that time.

    Visits to Ye Olde Magic Shoppe are abstracted by default, just like all other shopping in the game, and the GM decides what’s available every time the PCs return to Town from adventuring.

    The most noticeable class of enchantments here are your standard “plusses”: to-hit, damage and Parry for weapons, Defense Bonus and Block for shields, Defense Bonus and DR for armor. Each of these is a separate enchantment and has one or two levels available in the table.

    Weapons can also benefit from one of a set of elemental damage adders (flaming, lightning, freezing), from a Penetrating enchantment that helps get past DR, and from being made magically unbreakable. Armor and shields can have their weight reduced, and anything can be made glowy so that it illuminates like a torch on command.

    That’s it for purchasable magic gear. Want something more powerful or with a different effect? Go delve a dungeon and find something from the expanded GM-only list from Exploits.

    Magic As a Service

    The typical delver party arrives in town after a delve carrying a cartload of precious loot, and also a bevy of wounds, curses, severed limbs or even the dead bodies of their companions who didn’t make it. Fortunately, they can “generously donate” some of that treasure to the local temple in order to solve their other problems.

    HP is super cheap to recover at $2/point. Mundane and supernatural afflictions cost $50 and $100 respectively, which is also pretty cheap assuming you managed to find any loot at all. Things get more expensive when it comes to repairing permanent crippled body parts ($750) or outright dismemberment ($1600). And bringing someone back from the dead costs $15000.

    PC clerics who know the right spells can do almost all of these in the field, but the possibility of paying to have it done automatically in town is still nice. Presumably restoring or regrowing limbs and eyes can still be done this way even if the PC cleric tried and failed, which is nice.

    The one exception here is resurrecting the dead, which only the town priest can do. I bet that’s why it costs so much more. No competition from PCs!

    This is actually present as a very small text box in the character sheet at the very end of the book, and I couldn’t find it anywhere else. It’s quite possible I’ll run into the official explanation in some other book as I go through them.

    Let's Read DFRPG Adventurers: Misc. Gear

    If you’re like me, your eyes tend to sorta skip over the equipment section that comes after armor and weapons. Today, I’m going to have to stop and read it with more attention, as it’s still fairly important here. As I’ve said before, every last pound of gear matters in the DFRPG.

    The section of Chapter 7 that covers Miscellaneous Gear is split into three subsections, and we’ll cover all three here.

    Some pieces of gear in this chapter are described as “basic equipment” for a certain skill. This means you need them to use that skill normally, and if you’re forced to improvise or go without you’ll face a daunting -5 or -10 penalty. Items that are high-quality might give a +1 or +2 instead.

    Unlike weapons and armor, each item here gets at most one or two lines about its function and rules, followed by a cost and weight. They each have their uses, though.

    Basics

    This is the kind of stuff that’s relatively inexpensive and available at any town worthy of the name. There are many small sections here that I will discuss in summary instead of reprinting the contents of the book.

    Camping and Survival Gear contains tents, canteens, blankets, backpacks, and all that good stuff that often gets overlooked. Of note here are the Personal and Group Basics items, which are an abstract ball of knicknacks that are basic equipment for Survival. The first one is for a single person, containing stuff like utensils and some basic fire-making items. The group version has cookpots, a rope, a utility hatchet, and other things you need to help set up camp for 3-8 people. It weights 20 lbs but you can distribute its weight among several PCs. These don’t include shelter, so you might also want tents and bedrolls or sleeping furs. This is also where you find trail rations.

    Combat Accessories has a selection of different quivers, but also a sturdy belt that can support 2 or 3 melee weapons hanging from it, lanyards to mitigate those pesky “weapon dropped” rolls, and hobnailed boots that let you ignore poor footing but make a lot of noise. If you need more than 2-3 weapons ready to draw at all times, wear more belts! I think that’s how D&D 3e’s fashion came about.

    Containers are your chests, barrels and buckets. In my experience you tend to find those in dungeons more often than you need to buy them. There are also stats for an empty crystal vial of the sort that usually contains potions, and which you could use to collect samples of corrosive goo and the like.

    Light Sources covers expensive beeswax and stinky tallow candles, as well as lanterns and torches. Detailed rules for lighting are in Exploits.

    Medical Gear has bandages which are basic equipment for First Aid, a proper first-aid kit which gives +1, a liter for carrying the wounded, and surgical equipment that can be used for field Surgery or to remove valuable dead monster bits without ruining them.

    Miscellaneous Hardware is, yes, the miscellanea among the miscellanea. Various ropes, chains, a ladder, and poles in both six- and ten-foot varieties.

    Musical Instruments are for bards and for anyone else who wants to make noise, which requires no skill. There’s a “baseline” two-handed stringed instrument which can be anything from an ancient lyre to a modern acoustic guitar. Woodwinds are lighter but you can’t speak while using them. A harp or zither is more expensive but can be played quietly enough to not be heard through a door. And if you’re more of a bard-barian type horns, drums, and bagpipes can be heard for miles!

    Tools is a large category that has your crowbars, hammers, saws, pickaxes and shovels. It also has a utility machete that’s crappy as a weapon but useful for cutting foliage, and more specialized things like tongs, scales, and even a backpack toolkit for field armorers.

    Writing Equipment has somewhat anachronistic sheets of paper in bundles of 20, a scribe’s kit with quill, inkwell and penknife, and a humble piece of chalk for writing on dungeon walls.

    Special Orders

    You can start with these when creating a character, but acquiring them in play requires finding a specialist vendor that deals with them (alchemist, eccentric artificer, smuggler, etc.).

    Adventure-Wear covers a large set of specialized and somewhat anachronistic fashion, like the Delver’s Webbing that works like a modern tactical harness full of pouches and pockets, or helmet lamps.

    Camping Gear has alchemical matches, a compass, a timed candle that burns at a precise fixed rate, and special rations. Elven rations are very expensive but three times lighter. Dwarven ones weight twice as much as the standard human stuff but give you poison resistance after eating them for a month or more.

    Combat Accessories here cover bandoleers for those people who absolutely must carry 24 throwing daggers on them, a variety of crossbow accessories, and even lamps and lecterns that you can bolt onto your shield.

    Covert Ops and Security is known as “thief gear” in other games. A very large section that includes everything from your classic set of lockpicks and trap-disarming tools to dungeon camouflage and both smoke and flash bombs for those who love a good ninja vanish move. It’s also where you find stats for padlocks, which again are something you will run into in the dungeon more than you’ll need to buy.

    Esoteric Supplies includes the healing kits for the cleric’s and druid’s special skills, and your usual selection of holy symbols and holy water.

    Optics has a bull’s eye lantern that projects a long beam instead of a small circular light, periscopes, telescopes, and mirrors.

    Scholarly Gear includes professional cartography kits, waterproof scroll cases, self-righting hourglasses, as well as manuals and tomes that can let you roll at default for knowledge skills you don’t have, or give a bonus to those you do.

    Concoctions

    These aren’t magical, but they follow the same rules as Special Order gear, and so might not be easily available after character creation.

    Chemicals are mostly throwable grenades that burst on impact. They can be filled with acid, alchemist’s fire, glue, or visibility dust. There are also glow vials which illuminate as much as a torch when activated but don’t use fire.

    Natural Preparations include a selection of drinkable druidic anti-toxins, and bundles of garlic and wolfsbane for use against vampires and werewolves.

    Poisons are fantasy poisons that act fast enough to affect combat, dealing some amount of damage at once when the victim fails a resistance roll. They’re too fast for anti-toxins, which only help against stuff with ongoing effects. Some are utility compounds that you spread on your bladed weapons, others are grenades. They range from the very weak to the terrifyingly deadly.

    Impressions

    I ended up reading this section more closely than I had in years, which is a good thing! A lot of the basic items here are stuff that remains useful throughout a whole campaign, as they can’t easily be replaced with spells or magic items.
