    Let's Read DFRPG Exploits: Chapter 2, Dungeon Delving

    This is the chapter that answers the one big question everyone when reading a game for the first time: what do we do here?

    And the answer ends up being quite minimalistic: your delver group starts in Town, where they use their free time to find a dungeon to raid and prepare an expedition. Then they travel to that dungeon, fight monsters, loot treasure, and return to Town to spend it on upgrades and prepare for their next delve.

    This reminds me of old-school D&D campaigns, which were more or less like this for a lot of editions of the game. It also reminds me of the first Diablo game, which unlike D&D is mentioned by Sean Punch as one of the overt inspirations for DFRPG.

    And yeah, you can hang a meatier narrative on top of these bones, but as in those old D&D editions that is entirely up to you. I’m personally fine with that, as coming up with this stuff was never a problem for me.

    In Town

    The first big section starts with a description of the nature and purpose of the abstract “Town” used throughout the text. As mentioned in previous posts, “Town” is a shorthand for whatever settlement the PCs visit between dungeon expeditions. Whether it’s always the same place or a different one every time, Town is supposed to be safe and actually kinda “boring”: this isn’t where adventure happens, this is where you prepare for adventure.

    Our platonic ideal of a Town has at least one inn where our delvers can live between expeditions, a tavern where they can learn rumors, and various places that can sell stuff to the PCs and buy their loot. These are mostly abstracted as “Guilds” (Merchants’, Wizards’, Thieves’), but you also have the Temple that provides healing services and evil artifact disposal. The Town Watch keeps the thieves in check and maintains the peace, and the King’s Men swoop in to take care of bigger problems if necessary.

    This is all in keeping with the extremely generic and tongue-in-cheek implied setting I talked about in the Adventurers reading. Every game report I’ve seen had something that was more fleshed out than this, but the books leave it very much as an exercise to the reader.

    Anyway, if adventure happens Out There, what can you do In Here?

    The sections that follow list several possibilities, with related skill rolls attached to each. These rolls are assumed to be “montages”, representing what you spend the whole week doing. Some of them are risky and might even damage you! In that case you start the next adventure proper with that harm marked on your sheet, and have to deal with it in play.

    Getting Stuff Cheap

    When you create a character, you just buy stuff from the Equipment chapter in Adventurers at list price. This is also the default when you go shopping in Town, but you can also use some of the handy tricks described here to try to get them at a discount. According to the book, true munchkins will demand the GM start the very first adventure in Town so that they can score some extra gear this way.

    Possibilities can range from haggling and bargain hunting to making your own weapons and concoctions, trying to dodge taxation by buying things in the black market, all the way to the good old five finger discount you get from shoplifting. All of them come with required skill rolls and suggested modifiers.

    Scoring Extra Cash

    If you do start out in town like a true munchkin, or if pickings have been slim lately, you might find yourself without a lot of money while waiting for your next quest. This section has some ways of earning spare change in town. And I do mean spare change: none of them pay remotely as well as actual adventuring. You do this when you have to get those last few coppers you need to pay for this week’s inn bill or for that one extra potion.

    From least to most profitable we have: looking for lost change in the mud; begging or performing for coin on the street; gambling; picking people’s pockets; and debasing coin. That last bit is a funny historical crime where you scrape the precious metal off the sides of real coins and use the shavings to make fake ones by mixing them with base metal.

    The money you get is proportional to your margin of success, and failures can bring negative consequences. Rummaging through the mud risks disease. Performing, gambling and stealing risk beatings, with a critical failure on the last one meaning they break your hand as punishment. Getting caught counterfeinting the King’s coin means the King’s Men dip your hand in molten silver, which is a particularly cruel way of inflicting the One Hand disadvantage.

    What’s that? Getting an honest job? No, we don’t do that here.

    Finding a Quest

    The GM of course is expected to have one or more possible adventures planned out when running a game. That doesn’t mean the PCs will automatically know about them. This is how they can find out.

    They can use several different skills to chase down rumors, or to advertise their services to prospective quest givers. Once they have a lead, they can hit the books or hire sages to research the location in more detail.

    And if all else fails there’s the charmingly named option of starving, which is the first time this book talks about cost of living. The subject actually first came up on the equipment section of Adventurers, but I ended up missing it because it was in a box off to the side.

    The typical assumption here is that delvers stay at inns when in Town. The total cost for their room and board come out to $150 per week. They must pay this for every week they stay in town, which means things can get tight if they take too long to find a dungeon. PCs might skip this cost if they get free room and board for some reason, and outdoorsy types can choose roll either Survival to live off the land (or Urban Survival to live off the streets…) each week to avoid payments as well. However, failure there meakes you take damage from starvation and other hazards, and you’ll start your next adventure missing that much HP.

    Finding a Sponsor

    Whether you found a dungeon yourself or were handed a quest, you can try to negotiate with wealthy investors for extra funding. The party chooses one envoy to make the appropriate influence rolls, and on a success they gain a gear allowance of $100 x the margin of success. The sponsor will however expect payment in the form of looted treasure whose value is triple that of the gear allowance after the party returns. They might settle for a particular item instead. Surely giving away that plain gold ring with gibberish written on it is a better deal than parting with your coins, right?

    Coming Back Rich

    So you’ve been to the dungeon and now you’re back with a big stack of loot weighing you down. What do you do with it?

    Keepers

    You’ll want to keep some of that stuff, particularly if it’s cool gear or useful consumables. Several classic approaches are mentioned, which I guess is useful for people who are playing a game like this for the first time. Splitting by shares, “to each according to their need” (a fine way to maximize party power), and even “you get what you manage to grab”, with a snide little aside that Thieves often play by this rule regardless of what the party chose.

    A more important note warns us that PCs can’t affect each other with social skills - any negotiation here happens between the players themselves. I’d also include mind control spells as off-limits here, though I would probably not want to play with a group where I needed to make this explicit.

    We end with a paragraph on adjusting armor, since it’s a bit of a special case when it comes to loot. Armor made for someone of a different Size Modifier (i.e, human armor when you’re a halfling or vice-versa) will never fit no matter what you do. A suit made for someone of the same SM can be adjusted to fit you with an Armory (Body Armor) skill roll. This is useful for people with different builds, for dwarves who want to wear human-made armor (or vice-versa), and for anyone who wants to wear a suit of Fine armor made for someone else, which is harder to modify than usual.

    Getting a Good Price

    Anything you don’t want to keep, you want to sell in order to get money and buy better gear. “Perpetual” dungeons that are too vast for anyone to clear or which rearrange and restock themselves periodically can have merchant enclaves right outside the entrance or even inside the dungeon itself, ready to buy the haul from any party who reaches them. If that’s not the case, then the stuff has to be hauled back to town to be sold, which can be a logistical challenge.

    Coins are always worth their full value - they’re already money! The same goes for bulk precious metals like gold ingots or the like, as they’re more or less worth their weight in coins. For everything else you start with the lower of actual value or what the seller believes it’s worth, and multiply it by a percentage dependent on the seller’s Wealth. Average Wealth, the default, means you sell loot at 40% of its base value. Being richer means you get higher percentages because you have better connections, and being poorer means the opposite.

    You can further adjust this by having a very high reaction bonus, haggling with the Merchant skill, or turning to the black market with Streetwise. Success at any of these increases your effective Wealth by one level, while failure tends to decrease it. The Town’s temple will also take in appropriate goods (like relics and incense) at a better price, with no rolls necessary, though it pays in equipment or healing credit rather than hard cash. I’d also include “evil artifacts for disposal” here.

    There are sections here on how to deal with parties who drag every rusty hinge and chipped goblin sword back to town for sale (sell it as scrap by the wagon-load for a modest amount), and on how to earn a little extra by composing and selling the tale of your exploits. And finally, if you were on a quest, this is where you get paid for it.

    There and Back Again

    This is the section that covers getting to the dungeon and then back to Town. Travel time is usually whatever the GM proclaims. They might arrive at a number by calculating distances on a map, rolling randomly, or even just shouting “40 days and 40 nights!” whenever someone asks.

    The party must bring or forage 3 meals for every day of travel time per PC, and every day also brings a random encounter roll. There are several ways to reduce that time from the base figure given by the GM: PCs can roll Navigation to find the best path, Weather Sense to avoid bad weather, and one of several travel skills like Hiking or Riding to make better time. Each success knocks off 10% from the base time or 30% on a crit.

    You also have rules for camping and for night ambushes by monsters: randomly determine whose watch they arrive at, and roll Perception or Observation for the lookout to figure out if the party is surprised. Against sneaky monsters, this is a Quick Contest against their Stealth. There are some things the PCs can do to make their camp harder to spot in the wilderness, or harder to approach inside a dungeon (traps!).

    Finally, you get a note on how to track someone through the wilderness as you travel.

    Coming Up Next

    Turns out the chapter on how to play the whole dang game is too big to summarize in one post. Who’d have thunk it? In the next post we’ll look at rules for actually delving the dungeon.

    Hell's Rebels Rebellion Rules for GURPS

    Back in Adventure 1, Scene 09 of my Let’s Play of Hell’s Rebels, I wrote up a quick and dirty rules conversion for the campaign’s rebellion rules. Now I’m putting them in their own post to make them easier to find in the future. I’ll make some edits, but they’ll likely remain just as quick and dirty as they were before.

    Rebellion Rules Intro

    These rules require the Hell’s Rebels Player’s Guide for Pathfinder, as the writeup just converts the tests. Still, I’ll try to give a brief explanation below.

    The general idea is that the Silver Raven rebellion has a “character sheet” with three main “skills” named Secrecy, Loyalty, and Security. These progress more or less like Pathfinder 1e saves, with the one chosen as the rebellion’s main focus using the “good save” progression and the others using the “bad save” progression.

    The rebellion also has a number of Supporters which acts as its “XP”. Supporters include both actual members and citizens of Kintargo who lend their support in more indirect ways. If the rebellion has enough, it “levels up” and its stats increase.

    Then you have the officer posts, six of them, which can be filled by PCs or allied NPCs. In the original rules officers would get to add their ability bonuses to certain rebellion skill tests. Here, they get to make complimentary skill tests for those same tasks.

    The rebellion starts with access to a small set of basic tasks, but to get access to the rest it must recruit NPC teams. Each team allows certain actions to be taken, and can be upgraded twice to allow more actions. The higher the organization’s level, the more teams it can support.

    On each strategic turn, which represents a week of game time, the organization can perform a number of actions determined by its level, and each has their own rules.

    Da Rules

    Basic Stats and Tests

    • The Security, Loyalty and Secrecy skills all start at 10. Pick one to be the focus as usual, and add the corresponding skill bonuses listed on p. 22 of the Player’s Guide to determine the actual skill level. So the focused skill starts at 12 and goes to 22, and the unfocused ones start at 10 and go to 16.

    • Check Difficulties: DC 10 checks translate into unmodified skill tests. Each +5 DC over 10 translates into a -2 penalty. So a DC 30 check, the hardest possible for end-game actions, would be at -8. Complementary bonuses are important, particularly to unfocused tests.

    • By the way I’m thinking of using a slight variation of this for personal tests during delves and such, with each +5 DC over 10 translating into a -1 penalty. This is more generous, but it applies to skills that might not increase as steadily over time and won’t have as many complimentary rolls applied to them.

    Level-up Awards

    Each rebellion level-up gives an award to the PCs. Possible awards are Cash, XP, Training, and Titles, all of which have their own rules in the original version. For GURPS, they are:

    • For Cash awards, multiply all gp amounts by 5 for the amount in GURPS dollars as usual.
    • XP awards gift a flat 15 character points.
    • Training awards let you put 4 character points towards any skill, or a mix of them if they’re at a low enough level.
    • Each title grants one level of Reputation (all the time, everyone but Thrune supporters) valued at 3 points/level.

    Officers

    Officers can use some of their skills as Complementary Skills for organization tests. If you have more than one of the listed skills you can test them all.

    Multiple people can occupy the same post, but only one gets to roll their skills for a given action.

    • Demagogue:
      • May use complimentary skills on Loyalty checks.
      • Skills: Propaganda, Public Speaking, Leadership.
    • Partisan:
      • May use complimentary skills on Security checks.
      • Skills: Strategy, Leadership, Tactics.
    • Recruiter:
      • May use complimentary skills on checks for recruiting supporters.
      • Adds rebellion rank to the number of recruited supporters, and this stacks with multiple recruiters.
      • Skills: Public Speaking, Streetwise, Diplomacy.
    • Sentinel:
      • Occupying this post gives a +1 to the rebellion’s non-focused skills.
      • May use complimentary skills on any check used to resolve an Event.
      • Skills: any of the ones listed for the appropriate category.
    • Spymaster:
      • Spymaster: May use complimentary skills on Secrecy checks.
      • Skills: Intelligence Analysis, Streetwise, Administration.
    • Strategist:
      • Occupying this position grants a bonus action. May use its complimentary skills on the bonus action.
      • Skills: Strategy, Tactics, Administration.

    Strategic Turns

    • Each turn represents a week of game time. The phases are as described in the books, run using the conversion notes above.

    Actions

    Actions are performed by teams. Each team can perform the actions in its description, using the appropriate Org Skill for it. Teams give a bonus to the Org Skill equal to their tier (this replaces the Team Manager mechanic).

    If the action has a monetary cost, that cost must be paid from the rebellion’s treasury.

    I’m not sure I will use the rules for the city’s magic item market and its limits, but the relevant actions are included here anyway.

    Action List:

    • Activate Black Market (Black Marketeers): Roll Secrecy -4 and spend $250. On success, the Ravens set up a black market for 1 week on Kintargo. Availability of magic items and special orders increases at the GM’s discretion. Items sold to the black market sell for an extra 10% of their base value (stacking with the Wealth-based percentage and the add-on for a successful Merchant roll).

    • Activate Safe House (Saboteurs, Spies, or Thieves): Requires no test. Choose a building on any neighborhood except for the Greens or the Castle District. That location becomes a safe house for 1 week. Caches placed at safe houses can’t be discovered by Thrune. It’s also a good place to stash rescued prisoners and to rest without fear of random patrols. Each active safe house gives a +1 bonus to Security (+5 max).

    • Change Officer Role (no team required): One PC can change their role in the Ravens.

    • Covert Action (Spies): Works automatically to aid the next rebellion action, granting a +3 to the test and reducing Notoriety gain to the minimum possible. Can also be used to place stashes or contacts at “tactical” delve sites, which have custom effects depending on the delve and stay around for 1 week.

    • Dismiss Team (no team required): Removes a team from the org and frees up their slot. Roll Loyalty; on a failure, gain 1d4 notoriety.

    • Earn Gold (Black Marketeers, Merchant Lords, Merchants, Peddlers): Roll Security and add $ equal to (Margin of Success * Team Tier * 10) to the org’s treasury. Failures earn no money, critical failures add 1d6 notoriety.

    • Gather Information (Agitators, Cognoscenti, Rumormongers, Street Performers): Roll Secrecy -2 with a bonus equal to team tier to gather a piece of information. This can be a rumor or a clue related to a specific investigation.

    • Guarantee Event (no team required): Kick a hornet’s nest or two, increase your Notoriety by 1d6, and guarantee an event that turn. The GM rolls twice and takes the lower result to find out what it is.

    • Knowledge Check (Cognoscenti): Roll a Secrecy test instead of whatever knowledge skill would be required to answer a specific question, using the same dificulty penalties. Kinda like a use of Contacts.

    • Lie Low (no team required): Take no other actions this turn and reduce your Notoriety by the number of teams in the org.

    • Manipulate Events (Cabalists): Like Guarantee Event, but better. The GM rolls twice and the cabalists get to choose which event happens. Notoriety remains unchanged. Add +3 to any rolls to resolve the event.

    • Recruit Supporters (no team required): Roll Loyalty, aided by complimentary rolls from Recruiters. Success recruits 2d6 + Recruiter Bonuses supporters. Critical failure raises notoriety by 1d6.

    • Recruit Team (no team required): Attempt to recruit a team, rolling the test and paying the cost demanded by their description, if any.

    • Refresh Marketplace (Black Marketeers, Merchant Lords, Merchants): Remove currently available magic items and reroll them. If you used a tier 3 team for this, you can re-roll one additional item. Not sure if I’m using these rules, but here they are.

    • Rescue Character (Cabalists, Infiltrators, Spellcasters): The team attempts to rescue one captured character. Roll a Security check with a penalty determined by the GM. On a success, the character is rescued and sent to the org’s HQ or a safe house. Increase Notoriety by 2d6 on a success or 1d6 on a failure (which doesn’t rescue the character). Note that certain rescues must be performed by the PCs as a mission and can’t be replaced with this task.

    • Reduce Danger (Freedom Fighters, Infiltrators, Cabalists, Spellcasters): Roll Security -2 and reduce the city’s Danger for this week and the next by 5 + Margin of Success. Failure instead increases Notoriety by 1d4 and Danger by 5 for the same amount of time.

    • Restore Character (Spellcasters): This lets you use services from the Healing table on the DFRPG character sheet on a character, for the listed cost, paid out of the treasury.

    • Sabotage (Saboteurs): Roll Secrecy -4 to give a +2 bonus to all other org checks this week and reduce your notoriety by 2d6. Failure reduces your supporters and Kintargo’s population by 2d6. Can also be used for custom actions in certain delves.

    • Secure Cache (Saboteurs, Sneaks, Spies, Thieves): Roll Secrecy to stash a number of items at a location in Kintargo. Buy the items if you don’t already have them. Penalty, weight, and cost limits depend on the size of the cache: Minor (-2, 2.5kg, $4500), Intermediate (-4, 5kg, $12500), or Major (-8, 10kg, no limit). Planting a cache deep inside enemy territory might add another -2 or -4 penalty. Certain custom cache locations might be available with their own rules.

    • Special (no team required): For actions which must be taken to resolve events and the like. Each has their own special rules.

    • Special Order (Merchant Lords): Buy a magic item that’s more expensive than the city’s current limit, at a 5% discount and with a 2d6 day wait. You can pay an extra $5000 to reduce wait time to 1 day.

    • Spread Disinformation (Agitators, Cognoscenti, Rumormongers): Roll Secrecy -4. On a success, decrease Notoriety by 1d6, and an additional 1d6 for every 2 MoS. On a failure, increase it by 1d6.

    • Upgrade Team (no team required): Pay a team’s upgrade cost and upgrade it one tier. A single team can only be upgraded once a week, but multiple actions can be used to upgrade multiple teams.

    Conclusion

    That sure is one huge list of GURPS rules. I’ll keep this updated as I change it, and will use it as a reference for running the strategic side of the rebellion. With this I have what I need for my next step: running a few rebellion turns to get our organization to level 5 before we start Adventure 2.

    Let's Read DFRPG Exploits: Chapter 1, Rolling the Dice

    Chapter 1: Rolling the Dice

    A lot of this is stuff we already saw in my introduction post to Adventurers, but we go into more detail here. Check that post for the dice notation, the definition of success, reaction, and damage rolls, and Quick Contests.

    Success Rolls

    As mentioned earlier, success rolls are done using three dice with the aim of rolling a number equal to or less than your effective skill level for a given task. Your margin of success or failure is the difference between that skill level and what you rolled.

    Effective skill level means your base level in the relevant skill or attribute adjusted by any bonuses or penalties that apply in the present situation. A roll of 3 or 4 is always a critical success, a 18 always a critical failure. 5 or 6 can be critical successes if your effective skill level is 15 or 16 respectively. A 17 is a critical failure if your ESL is 15 or less, and a normal failure if it’s 16 or more.

    The first bit of new material here is a box about who rolls and how. Players roll for their PC’s actions, of course, and they always roll in the open. GMs roll for everyone else in the world, and should also roll in the open unless the action in question is happening entirely outside the knowledge of the PCs, or when the outcome isn’t something they’d know right away.

    We also get two rules for teamwork: Complimentary Skills and Group Tests. Complimentary skills let a PC make a roll for the same or a related skill to help another’s attempt. A success grants a +1 bonus, a failure a -1 penalty, and criticals in either direction double that. A group test is for when all the PCs are trying to do the same thing as a team, such as sneak past a monster or survive in the wilderness. The PC with the highest skill rolls, with a -1 penalty for each other PC who doesn’t have the skill. Everyone shares in the result.

    We also get rules for Time Spent: when you’re attempting something that takes a certain amount of time by default, each doubling of that time gives you a +1 to your roll. On the other hand, if you’re hurrying, each 10% reduction of the standard time gives you a -1. Yes, you can roll to pick that lock instantly at -10.

    Repeated attempts get their on subsection. Stuff where repeated attempts are the norm (like attacking in combat) get no penalty. If failure injures you, you can try again after a short time without penalty other than risking more injury. If it doesn’t injure you, you instead get a cumulative -1 penalty per repeated attempt. And finally, if failure kills you, you obviously don’t get to try again…

    Contests

    As seen earlier, a Quick Contest is when both opponents roll once and compare their margins of success. The one with the greatest marging wins. The roll doesn’t have to be against the same skill for both participants. For example, a wizard can roll against their spell skill and the spell’s target can roll against their Will or HT to resist, and that’s still a Quick Contest.

    One new thing that will come up a lot here is the Rule of 16, and it applies mostly in situations like the example above. In spellcasting or magical attacks, the attacker’s skill level is usually much higher than the defender’s HT or Will, which can be a bit unfair. This rule caps the caster’s effective skill at 16 or the defender’s resisting attribute, whichever is higher, to make it less of a foregone conclusion. So a wizard casting a sleep spell with skill 20 at a Will 9 orc will roll a contest of 16 vs. 9. If it was a Will-18 greater demon instead, it would be 18 vs. 18.

    We also have Regular Contests, which are the boring version of Quick Contests where both sides roll until one succeeds and the other fails. It’s theoretically meant to model prolonged disputes, but I never ran into an occasion where using this rule would make things more fun.

    Sense Rolls

    This takes up a whole page, and explains GURPS’ take on the classic adventurer activity of “rolling to notice stuff”. You always roll Perception for this, as expected, but the GM decides which of the character’s senses is most relevant to the roll, and applies relevant modifiers. This starts with the proper Acute (Sense) advantage, and then goes through several penalties relevant to that sense. Vision cares a lot about illumination, Hearing about volume and intervening obstacles, and so on.

    If more than one sense matters, I imagine you make separate rolls for each. These are usually entirely handled by the GM because a PC wouldn’t know if they “failed to notice” something in the fiction.

    Influence Rolls

    These represent deliberate attempts to influence someone’s opinion, as opposed to just letting your good looks and melodious voice do the work for you. Instead of making a reaction roll, roll a Quick Contest between your most applicable social skill and the other person’s Will. The same modifiers that would apply to the other person’s reaction roll now modify your skill. A success means a Good reaction, and a failure generates a Bad one. If you’re a skilled talker, this can be slightly safer than relying on reaction rolls, which can get you both much better and much worse results.

    By default you can only try to influence people, but there are advantages that let you do it to nonsapient animals, spirits with alien mentalities, or even plants.

    Fright Checks

    These are Will rolls to resist fear, but they’re also a bit of a special case. Your Will is modified by a host of courage-related advantages and disadvantages, then by how scary the thing causing the fear is, and then capped at 13. Failing leaves you mentally stunned for seconds equal to the margin of failure, which is potentially lethal in combat.

    Failing also forces you to roll on a table to see if you develop long-term trauma in the form of new mental disadvantages. These reduce your point value, but can be bought off with earned points.

    The Unfazeable advantage lets you ignore all this, as is traditional for GURPS.

    Reaction Rolls

    We already explained these back in the Adventurers introduction: roll 3d with modifiers applying directly to the rolled result, higher is better.

    There are some new notes about how monsters react to PC delvers. As with NPCs, logic is sovereign; if you’re sure a monster would attack on sight, or behave in some other way, do that. If you’re not sure, you roll. However, monsters have some heavy reaction penalties if the PCs are invading their territory, if they’re supernaturally compelled to guard an area, or if they’re particularly alien or evil in nature. They all stack, and in your typical dungeon delve at least one will apply to most denizens the PCs encounter.

    As mentioned earlier, reaction rolls can be replaced by Influence rolls if appropriate.

    Urist Stortebeker, the Last Archivist

    An update to Urist Stortebeker, one of the player characters for my solo Hell’s Rebels campaign. His description and backstory remain the same as his starting version, and we discuss what changed here.

    I tried leading Urist in a “fightier” direction, as befits an axe-wielding dwarf, but my success was mixed here. He’s generally hard to hurt and his new armor should make him even more so, but he hasn’t been the most successful at hurting enemies. That said, his healing spells have been literal life-savers here, and his buff and utility magic has pulled its weight.

    He started at 250 points and is now at 294, with zero unspent points. This is a higher total than the others because I noticed I had too many disadvantages on his starting sheet and removed one. I don’t believe it will cause any imbalance.

    That said I did try to make him fightier. One level each in Axe/Mace, Shield, ST, and Higher Purpose (Slay Demons) make him hit better and harder, doubly so against demons. He can also wear heavier armor.

    He also improved or acquired several skills that help run the rebellion’s strategic layer. Magic-wise, we have a couple of levels of Energy Reserve to help with the casting, which is slightly better for that purpose than more Fatigue because it recovers in parallel.

    Let's Read DFRPG Exploits: Introduction

    If DFRPG: Adventurers was our “Player’s Handbook” with everything you need to create a character, then Expoits is our “Dungeon Master’s Guide” with everything you need to actually play the game, and everything your GM needs to challenge and reward you. Or, well, almost. There’s a separate book for spells and another one for monsters, and we’ll get to them in due time.

    Cover

    Its cover is the image to the right, above. I’m putting both of them here again because I like the effect they have when put side by side.

    Quick Rules Reference

    This sheet here can probably be printed to become a second panel in your GM’s screen. It has stuff that’s handy for GMs this time around. We start with a reference on Light and Vision, listing what penalties you get to see in various levels of illumination. Daylight is a 0, total darkness is a -10, and every number in-between is possible.

    Next is a list of light sources PCs are likely to carry into a dungeon, along with their areas of effect, which can either be a circular radius or a tight beam. They elimiate darkness penalties in this area, and reduce the penalty to -3 up to 3x that distance.

    Then you get quick tables to figure out falling damage based on how far you fall, how far you can throw heavy stuff based on your Basic Lift, and a complete table listing the damage modifiers for every damage type, hit location, and enemy “constitution” (Living, Unliving, or Homogenous). This will all make sense when we get to the combat chapters.

    The Actual Introduction

    Despite the comparison above, players are encouraged to read some of Exploits too, mainly the parts containing the basic rules of the game that explain how they can do stuff. The rest of the book is more useful to the GM when planning campaigns and adventures, but there’s no iron-clad Gygaxian prohibition against players reading that too.

    The page finishes with recommendations on where to get more material. There are several supplements for DFRPG specifically that came out after the core box, but even at the time of its release you could choose to branch out to “mainline GURPS” if you wished, and to the “sister line” of GURPS Dungeon Fantasy supplements.

    Impressions

    Not a lot to have impressions about, though I do like that the illumination rules get a bit of spotlight here. Those tend to be very important for any game that features lots of dungeon delving.

    We’ll, er, delve deeper into damage types in the Combat chapter, but I’ll say that having them here for several types of enemy physiology is a great help as well. Few genres feature more enemies with bizarre anatomies than dungeon fantasy.
